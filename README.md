# Paul Graham's Principles for Clear Thinking

A distillation of the core principles embedded across Paul Graham's essays — synthesized into a small number of rock-solid ideas that apply to thinking, work, and life.

This is not a summary. It is a compression. The goal is the same as PG's own goal in writing: to find the most essential thing, and say it as plainly as possible.

## 1. Curiosity Is the Engine, Not the Fuel

**The principle:** Follow what you are excessively curious about — curious to a degree that would bore most other people. That is your signal. Not prestige. Not money. Not what seems promising.

**The failure mode it prevents:** Choosing work based on what looks impressive or financially safe rather than what genuinely engages you. This is a catastrophic error because external motivators are always worse guides than intrinsic interest. They point at the wrong things. They run out.

**Why it works:** Genuine curiosity is self-reinforcing — it increases rather than sates when indulged. It also provides a substitute for willpower: you do not need discipline to keep going at something you cannot stop thinking about. And it leads you to unusual places, because the paths that look promising are already crowded. The paths that lead to new discoveries tend to look unpromising. Only genuine curiosity keeps you on them long enough to find what is there.

**The test:** What do you think about when you are not trying to think about anything? That is what you are actually curious about. What you think you *should* be curious about is almost always wrong.

**Source essays:** [How to Do Great Work](https://paulgraham.com/greatwork.html), [The Bus Ticket Theory of Genius](https://paulgraham.com/genius.html), [How to Think for Yourself](https://paulgraham.com/think.html), [Superlinear Returns](https://paulgraham.com/superlinear.html), [Earnestness](https://paulgraham.com/earnest.html)

## 2. Optimize for the Thing, Not the Metric

**The principle:** There is always a difference between the thing you care about and the metric used to measure it. Once you optimize for the metric, you stop getting better at the thing.

**The failure mode it prevents:** The most dangerous form of wasted effort is effort that *looks* like progress. Getting a good grade in a class is so different from learning the subject that you have to choose one or the other. Appearing thorough is different from being right. Writing that sounds sophisticated is different from writing that conveys an idea. These pairs look similar from the outside. They are opposite from the inside.

**Why it works:** Metrics are always proxies — necessarily incomplete representations of what actually matters. Any system optimized for a proxy eventually diverges from the real goal. The divergence is invisible precisely because the metric is still going up.

**The test:** Ask: am I doing this because it is the right thing, or because it will *look* like the right thing? If the answer would change depending on whether anyone was watching, you are optimizing for the metric.

**Source essays:** [The Lesson to Unlearn](https://paulgraham.com/lesson.html), [Write Simply](https://paulgraham.com/simply.html), [Earnestness](https://paulgraham.com/earnest.html), [How to Work Hard](https://paulgraham.com/hwh.html)

## 3. Identity Makes You Stupid

**The principle:** The more labels you attach to yourself, the dumber they make you. When a topic becomes part of your identity, you can no longer think clearly about it — you can only defend it.

**The failure mode it prevents:** Partisan thinking. Once you are a *Democrat*, a *Christian*, a *Rust programmer*, a *contrarian* — any claim that threatens that identity stops being a question to evaluate and becomes a threat to deflect. Your reasoning power is diverted from finding truth to defending territory. The worst part: you do not notice. People who think conformally almost never identify themselves as conformists.

**Why it works:** Clear thinking requires psychological distance from the object of thought. Identity eliminates that distance entirely. The fix is not to have no views — it is to hold them lightly and let go of the label. The one safe identity is *scientist*, because it commits you to a process (following evidence wherever it leads) rather than a fixed position.

**The test:** Can you steelman the strongest argument against your own position without feeling like you are betraying something? If not, identity has already compromised your thinking.

**Source essays:** [Keep Your Identity Small](https://paulgraham.com/identity.html), [How to Think for Yourself](https://paulgraham.com/think.html), [What You Can't Say](https://paulgraham.com/say.html)

## 4. What You Work On Matters More Than How Hard You Work

**The principle:** The choice of problem is the most important decision you make. Working hard on the wrong thing is not neutral — it is actively harmful, because it displaces the right thing.

**The failure mode it prevents:** Type B procrastination — the most dangerous kind, because it looks like productivity. You are getting things done. Just the wrong things. Checking email, attending meetings, clearing your inbox: these are not rest and they are not work. They are a third category, and they expand to fill any space you give them. Meanwhile, the important project sits untouched, because important projects are terrifying and errands are not.

**Why it works:** There is only one "top idea" in your mind at any given time — the one your thoughts drift toward when they are free. Whatever occupies that position gets the benefit of your unconscious processing: the ideas that arrive in the shower, the connections that form on walks. The wrong top idea is a disaster, because it captures your best thinking for nothing.

Life is also genuinely short. Not philosophically short — quantifiably short. The right test for whether something deserves your time is whether you will care about it in five years. Most things you spend time on, you will not.

**The test:** What problem are you actually working on — the one you think about when your mind is free, not the one on your calendar? Is that the most important thing you could be doing right now?

**Source essays:** [The Top Idea in Your Mind](https://paulgraham.com/top.html), [Good and Bad Procrastination](https://paulgraham.com/procrastination.html), [Life is Short](https://paulgraham.com/vb.html), [How to Do Great Work](https://paulgraham.com/greatwork.html), [Fashionable Problems](https://paulgraham.com/fp.html)

## 5. An Idea Is Not Finished Until It Is Written

**The principle:** Putting an idea into words is not how you express what you think — it is how you find out what you think. The idea that feels complete in your head is almost never complete.

**The failure mode it prevents:** False confidence in your own understanding. Ideas feel finished from the inside. Writing exposes the seams — the places where you assumed a connection that does not hold, where you elided a step that does not work. "Half the ideas that end up in an essay will be ones you thought of while you were writing it." The other half will have changed beyond recognition. If you have not written about something, you have not finished thinking about it.

**Why it works:** A large portion of knowledge is unconscious. Experts have *more* unconscious knowledge than beginners, not less — they have automated more. Writing forces tacit knowledge to become explicit, where it can be examined, questioned, and corrected. The stranger test: read your own writing as if you are someone who does not already know what you meant. You will immediately see what is missing.

**The test:** Can you explain the idea to someone intelligent who knows nothing about the field, without jargon, in plain language? If not, you understand it less well than you think.

**Source essays:** [Putting Ideas into Words](https://paulgraham.com/words.html), [Write Simply](https://paulgraham.com/simply.html), [Beyond Smart](https://paulgraham.com/smart.html), [How to Do Great Work](https://paulgraham.com/greatwork.html)

## 6. Good Work Compounds — Start Before You Are Ready

**The principle:** Returns from work are not linear — they are superlinear. Each increment of learning makes the next easier. Each piece of real work creates the foundation for better work. The early phase always looks flat. Most people quit before the curve steepens.

**The failure mode it prevents:** Waiting until you are ready. You will never be ready, because readiness is itself a product of doing the work. The plan for a piece of work only works for achievements you can describe in advance. The best work is almost always discovered through successive versions: you start with something small and incomplete, and the work itself reveals what it needs to become. Releasing a crude version is not a compromise — it is the only way to learn what you are actually building.

**Why it works:** Knowledge grows fractally. From a distance, the edges of any field look smooth — complete, well-understood. Up close, every edge is full of gaps. The only way to find them is to get close enough. And the only way to get close enough is to start. "Whenever how well you do depends on how well you've done, you'll get exponential growth" — this applies to learning, to building, to everything that compounds.

**The test:** Is the reason you have not started yet a genuine constraint, or is it "big problems are terrifying"? The latter is almost always the answer.

**Source essays:** [Superlinear Returns](https://paulgraham.com/superlinear.html), [How to Do Great Work](https://paulgraham.com/greatwork.html), [Six Principles for Making New Things](https://paulgraham.com/newthings.html), [How to Get New Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/getideas.html), [Good and Bad Procrastination](https://paulgraham.com/procrastination.html)

## How These Principles Relate

They form a system, not a list.

**Curiosity** tells you what to work on. **Work on the right thing** tells you how to choose between options. **Identity** is the thing that corrupts both — it makes you believe you care about something when you are actually just defending it.

**Writing** is how you verify that your thinking is real. **Compounding** is what happens when real thinking accumulates over time. **Optimize for the thing** is the discipline that keeps all of it honest.

Violate any one of them and the others degrade. Choose work for prestige instead of curiosity — and the work becomes hollow. Let identity capture a belief — and writing becomes rationalization. Optimize for the metric — and compounding accelerates in the wrong direction.

## What This Is Not

This is a synthesis, not a substitute. Paul Graham's essays are worth reading in full — these principles are the skeleton, not the flesh. The arguments, examples, and texture of his writing are where most of the value lives.

This document tries to answer one question: *if you had to distill 20 years of PG's essays into the fewest possible ideas that actually change how you think*, what would they be?

Six seems close to right.

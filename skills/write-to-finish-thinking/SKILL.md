# An Idea Is Not Finished Until It Is Written

## The Principle

Putting an idea into words is not how you express what you think — it is how you find out what you think. The idea that feels complete in your head is almost never complete. Writing forces tacit knowledge to become explicit, where it can be examined, questioned, and corrected.

## When to Apply

Invoke this skill whenever the user is:

- Holding a strong view that has not been written out
- Making a decision based on reasoning that exists only in conversation
- Explaining something they understand but cannot articulate clearly
- Planning to act on a hypothesis they have not pressure-tested
- Saying "I just have a feeling that..." about something important

## The Failure Mode It Prevents

The dominant failure is *false confidence in your own understanding*. Ideas feel finished from the inside. You can stay in that feeling indefinitely without ever discovering the seams.

Writing exposes the seams immediately:
- Steps that you thought connected, and do not
- Assumptions you did not know you were making
- Words that sound meaningful but resolve to nothing concrete
- The edge of what you actually know, versus what you have absorbed and not examined

PG's claim is sharp: *no one who has not written about a topic has fully formed ideas about it.* Writing is the test. Without it, you cannot tell the difference between understanding and the comfortable feeling of understanding.

A related failure: experts have *more* unconscious knowledge than beginners, not less. They have automated more. This means experts especially benefit from writing, because the unconscious knowledge is exactly what others need to see and what the expert cannot transmit without first surfacing.

## How to Apply It in Conversation

When the user describes a position, analysis, or plan that has not been written:

1. **Ask them to commit it to text.** Not because writing is precious, but because verbalized reasoning skips steps that written reasoning catches. Offer to draft it together if useful.
2. **Apply the stranger test.** Have them imagine a thoughtful person who knows nothing about the context reading their argument. What is missing? What would not make sense?
3. **Translate jargon.** If the user cannot explain the idea in plain language without their field's vocabulary, the understanding is not as solid as it feels.
4. **Listen for the place the reasoning gets vague.** Vague language usually marks the boundary of actual understanding. That is where the writing exposes work that has not been done yet.

## What to Push Back On

- "I do not need to write this down — I know what I think" — this is precisely when writing is most needed. Confidence without written reasoning is the fingerprint of unexamined belief.
- "Writing it would just be paperwork" — paperwork is producing a document for someone else's process. Writing is producing a document for your own thinking. Different purpose, different value.
- "It is too obvious to need writing" — anything you cannot write down clearly is not actually obvious to you. It is obvious-feeling. That is different.
- "I will write it after I decide" — this is backwards. The writing *is* the deciding. Decisions made before writing are usually decisions that were made by something other than reasoning.

## The Test

> *Can you explain this idea, in plain language, to someone intelligent who knows nothing about the field, without using jargon you have not first defined?*

If not, you understand it less well than you think.

A second test:

> *When you read what you just wrote, does it surprise you anywhere?*

If yes, that is the writing doing its job. The surprise is where new understanding entered.

# What You Work On Matters More Than How Hard You Work

## The Principle

The choice of problem is the most important decision. Working hard on the wrong thing is not neutral — it is actively harmful, because it displaces the right thing. The right test for whether something deserves your attention is not whether it is on your list, but whether it is the most important thing you could be doing right now.

## When to Apply

Invoke this skill whenever the user is:

- Planning their day or week
- Reporting on what they have been busy with
- Prioritizing between competing demands
- Feeling productive but uneasy (a common symptom of working on the wrong thing)
- Avoiding a hard project by completing easy ones
- Describing their work as "putting out fires"
- Defining done for a project or sprint

## The Failure Mode It Prevents

The most dangerous failure mode is *Type B procrastination* — putting off the important thing by working on something less important. It is dangerous specifically because it does not feel like procrastination. The user is busy. Things are getting done. The to-do list is shrinking. Meanwhile, the important project is untouched, because important projects are terrifying and errands are not.

The deeper failure: there is only one *top idea* in the user's mind at any time — the idea their thoughts drift toward when free. Whatever occupies that position gets the benefit of unconscious processing: the ideas that arrive in the shower, the connections that form on walks. The wrong top idea is a disaster, because it captures the user's best thinking for nothing.

Disputes, conflicts, and minor anxieties are especially good at hijacking this position. They have the velcro-shape of genuinely interesting ideas without the substance.

## How to Apply It in Conversation

When the user reports their work, do not accept the list at face value. Probe:

1. **What is the top idea in your mind right now?** Not what *should* be — what actually is. The honest answer is what they think about in the shower. If it is not the most important thing they could be working on, that is the real problem.
2. **Which type of procrastination is happening?** Type A (doing nothing), Type B (doing the wrong thing), or Type C (postponing small stuff to do big stuff). Type C is good. Type B is the trap.
3. **What would you regret not doing if you looked back in 5 years?** Genuine importance has lasting resonance. Most things on the to-do list do not pass this test.
4. **What is being displaced?** The to-do list shows what they are doing. It does not show what they are not doing. The omission is usually where the real cost lives.

## What to Push Back On

- "I am too busy to work on X" — busy is a measurement of activity, not value. The question is whether the activity is the right activity.
- "I will get to it next week" — postponement of the important thing is itself the failure mode. Next week's calendar will have the same shape.
- "I had to handle Y first" — sometimes true, often a rationalization. Most "had to handle first" items are Type B procrastination wearing a uniform.
- "I have been making progress on Z" — progress on Z is only good if Z is the right thing. Progress on the wrong thing is regression on the right thing.

Push back most firmly when the user sounds productive but uneasy. The unease is data. It usually means the top idea in their mind is not what they have been working on.

## The Test

> *What problem occupies your mind when it is not occupied by anything?*

That is your top idea. Compare it to what your calendar says you are working on. If they do not match, the calendar is wrong.

A secondary test:

> *If you had to stop everything except the one most important thing, what would survive?*

That is the right thing. Everything else is competing for the attention it should be getting.
